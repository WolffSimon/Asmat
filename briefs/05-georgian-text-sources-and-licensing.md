# Brief 05: Georgian text sources, provenance and licensing

A research report on how much Georgian text exists, who wrote the text about Georgia, what can lawfully be used to train or ground a model, and how other projects have defined an insider. Compiled with AI research tools on 17 and 18 October 2026 from the sources cited in each paragraph. Each claim carries a label: peer-reviewed, preprint, vendor claim (including dataset and model cards), practitioner (NGO, media, government or community), or secondary (seen only in a summary or a citing page). **not found** marks a question the research could not answer. Tidied for publication on 18 October 2026: the report's sections on bias in model answers and on detecting perspective were left out, and the remaining sections were renumbered.

## Short answer

Georgian is about 0.05% of the open web. Every large open corpus of Georgian is a web crawl held outside Georgia, and the packaging licences on those corpora do not clear the copyright of the pages in them. The largest collections curated in Georgia, among them the Georgian National Corpus, Ilia State University's corpus and the National Library's 566,120 digitised documents, can be searched but not downloaded, and none carries an open licence. No study has measured how much of the text about Georgia was written by Georgians. No method of correcting for outsider-written training data has been evaluated on Georgian.

## 1. Measuring the corpus

### Georgian against Russian and English in the raw web

Common Crawl's own statistics (CLD2 on HTML pages, CC-MAIN-2026-39) count 908,943,016 English pages (41.8620%), 145,376,466 Russian (6.6954%) and 992,037 Georgian (0.0457%); Georgian ranks 47th of 162 detected languages, between Azerbaijani (0.0609%) and Armenian (0.0315%) [vendor/dataset card, Common Crawl Oct 2026, https://commoncrawl.github.io/cc-crawl-statistics/plots/languages; raw CSV https://raw.githubusercontent.com/commoncrawl/cc-crawl-statistics/master/plots/languages.csv]. Georgian first appears as a separately counted language in CC-MAIN-2018-34 (674,943 pages) and has been flat at roughly 0.9–1.5 million pages per crawl since 2020 [same source]. The resulting ratios (arithmetic on those figures) are Russian/Georgian 146.5 and English/Georgian 916.

The `.ge` top-level domain is 0.05% of URLs and 0.03% of hosts in the same crawl, against 4.94% and 2.84% for `.ru` (which folds in `.рф`) [vendor/dataset card, Common Crawl Oct 2026, https://commoncrawl.github.io/cc-crawl-statistics/plots/tld/latestcrawl.html]. The `.ge` URL share and the Georgian-language page share happen to be similar, but the populations overlap only partly: many `.ge` sites publish in English or Russian and much Georgian text sits on `.com` and `.org`.

### Georgian in the filtered corpora labs actually train on

Every open massively multilingual corpus places Georgian at roughly 0.04–0.07% of documents or words. FineWeb-2 holds 1,439,572,993 Georgian words in 3,706,659 documents (6.33 GB on disk) against 588,579,493,780 Russian words; English is not in FineWeb-2 [vendor/dataset card, Hugging Face read 17 Oct 2026, https://huggingface.co/datasets/HuggingFaceFW/fineweb-2]. That is 0.0431% of FineWeb-2 words and a Russian/Georgian ratio of 409 (derived from those figures). The FineWeb-2 paper uses GlotLID with per-language thresholds and does not discuss or filter machine-translated web content [preprint, arXiv Jun 2025, https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.20920].

CulturaX gives Georgian 3,120,321 documents and 2,617,625,564 tokens (0.04%) against 737,201,800,363 Russian and 2,846,970,578,793 English tokens [vendor/dataset card, Hugging Face, https://huggingface.co/datasets/uonlp/CulturaX]. mT5's mC4 table lists Georgian at 2.5 B tokens and 2.3 M pages (0.64% of the sampled mix after upsampling), Russian 713 B tokens, English 2,733 B [preprint, arXiv Oct 2020, https://arxiv.org/pdf/2010.11934]. OSCAR 23.01 has 1,343,036 Georgian documents and 373,935,158 words from a single late-2022 dump, with access currently gated and suspended [vendor/dataset card, Hugging Face, https://huggingface.co/datasets/oscar-corpus/OSCAR-2301]. XLM-R's CC-100 table gives Georgian 469 M tokens and 9.1 GiB uncompressed; the Hugging Face card lists the same subset as a 1.1 GB compressed download, so byte figures must always state compression [preprint, arXiv Nov 2019, https://arxiv.org/pdf/1911.02116; vendor/dataset card, https://huggingface.co/datasets/cc100].

HPLT 2.0 (cleaned) reports kat_Geor at 3.34e6 documents, 6.37e7 segments, 1.24e9 words and 1.02e10 characters, with sources mostly Internet Archive plus some Common Crawl [vendor/dataset card, https://huggingface.co/datasets/HPLT/HPLT2.0_cleaned]. HPLT 3.0 reports 6.13e6 documents, 1.06e8 segments, 7.55e9 tokens and 1.70e10 characters, with Russian at 4.40e12 and English at 1.63e13 tokens [vendor/dataset card, https://huggingface.co/datasets/HPLT/HPLT3.0]. The apparent six-fold jump from 1.24 B to 7.55 B is mostly a unit change: 2.0 counts whitespace words while 3.0 counts tokenizer tokens, and Georgian's three-byte Mkhedruli characters and agglutinative morphology fragment into many tokens. Like-for-like characters grew 1.7× (1.02e10 to 1.70e10) with 1.8× the documents. HPLT 3.0's Georgian share is 0.056% of its 13.5 T non-English tokens, with Russian/Georgian 583 and English/Georgian 2,157 (derived from those figures).

MADLAD-400's audit table keeps 936,497 Georgian documents ("ok") versus 402,458,746 Russian and 1,838,712,272 English; the per-language token count for Georgian was not in the extracted paper text: **not found** [vendor/dataset card, https://huggingface.co/datasets/allenai/MADLAD-400; preprint, arXiv Sep 2023, https://arxiv.org/pdf/2309.04662]. Glot500-c holds 991k kat_Geor rows and a separate 48.4k-row romanised kat_Latn subset, showing that Latin-script Georgian exists on the web and is invisible to script-based detection [vendor/dataset card, https://huggingface.co/datasets/cis-lmu/Glot500]. Georgian is absent from RedPajama-v2 (five languages), XGLM (30), BLOOM's published tables, Aya Expanse (23) and The Pile; among frontier model cards only Qwen3 lists Georgian by name (119 languages, ~36 T tokens) [vendor claim, Qwen3 blog, https://qwenlm.github.io/blog/qwen3/]. Llama 3 reports "8% multilingual tokens" without naming Georgian [preprint, arXiv Jul 2024, https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.21783]; Llama 4 and Gemma 3 cards do not mention it [vendor claim, https://huggingface.co/meta-llama/Llama-4-Scout-17B-16E-Instruct; https://huggingface.co/google/gemma-3-27b-it].

The spread in Georgian counts (0.37 B OSCAR words, 1.44 B FineWeb-2 words, 2.6 B CulturaX tokens, 7.5 B HPLT-3 tokens) comes from snapshot count (1 vs 96 vs ~150), unit (words vs characters vs subword tokens), filtering aggressiveness, and compression. Every figure should travel with its unit and snapshot range. Because Georgian's script has no confusion partner, its subsets are probably clean in language even when noisy in content; the risk is translated or boilerplate text, not mislabeled language.

### Books: even more skewed than the web

Google Books Ngram offers no Georgian corpus (it covers English variants, simplified Chinese, French, German, Hebrew, Spanish, Russian and Italian) [vendor claim, Google Books Ngram info, https://books.google.com/ngrams/info]. The Internet Archive's search API returns 1,009 text items tagged Georgian against 187,382 Russian and 37,894,754 English, of 52,325,154 texts; the language field is contributor-supplied and missing for many items, so these are lower bounds [vendor data via API, 18 Oct 2026, https://archive.org/advancedsearch.php?q=language%3A%28geo+OR+kat+OR+Georgian%29+AND+mediatype%3Atexts&fl%5B%5D=identifier&rows=0&output=json]. That is 0.0019% of IA texts versus 0.046% of Common Crawl pages (derived from those figures). HathiTrust by language, the Georgian national bibliography size and Books3/Anna's Archive language splits: **not found** (hosts blocked or budget exhausted).

### Wikipedia: edition sizes and how much is about Georgia

Georgian Wikipedia has 199,138 articles and 346 active users as of September 2026, against Russian 2,118,496 and 16,144 and English 7,241,803 and 263,530 [secondary, en.wikipedia "List of Wikipedias", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Wikipedias]. An earlier enwiki figure gives 192,841 Georgian articles on 8 April 2026, consistent with growth [secondary, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georgian_Wikipedia]. Ratios (derived from those figures): articles ru/ka 10.6, en/ka 36.4; active users ru/ka 46.7, en/ka 762; about 575 articles per active editor on kawiki versus 131 on ruwiki and 27 on enwiki, a thin maintenance base.

How much of English Wikipedia is about Georgia is answerable through the WP 1.0 assessment data: WikiProject Georgia (country) tracked 12,498 pages on 17 Oct 2026, of which 7,808 are article-class (FA 10, GA 44, B 218, C 655, Start 2,539, Stub 3,996, List 339, plus a few others); stubs are 51% of article-class pages, and the tracked set is 0.17% of enwiki [practitioner, WP1 API 17 Oct 2026, https://api.wp1.openzim.org/v1/projects/Georgia_(country)/table]. Comparators: WikiProject Russia 137,482 pages and WikiProject Azerbaijan 17,210 [practitioner, https://api.wp1.openzim.org/v1/projects/Russia/table; https://api.wp1.openzim.org/v1/projects/Azerbaijan/table]. English Wikipedia therefore over-covers Georgia (0.1–0.2%) relative to Georgian's share of the raw web (0.046%), by two to four times.

### Who writes about whom: the provenance literature

Hecht and Gergle measured self-focus in 15 editions and found it in 12, with English the most self-focused (ratio 7.2) [peer-reviewed, C&T 2009, https://collablab.northwestern.edu/pubs/CT2009-HechtAndGergle-SelfFocusBias.pdf]; across 25 editions more than 74% of concepts appear in only one [peer-reviewed, CHI 2010, https://arxiv.org/pdf/1904.01689]. The Oxford Internet Institute finds Africa with about 2.6% of geotagged articles against 14% of population [practitioner, OII geonet 21 Oct 2014, https://geonet.oii.ox.ac.uk/blog/the-geographically-uneven-coverage-of-wikipedia/] and Sub-Saharan Africa receiving 10.7 edits from outside for each it commits outward [practitioner summary of Graham et al. 2015, https://geonet.oii.ox.ac.uk/blog/informational-magnetism-on-wikipedia-geographic-networks-of-edits/; peer-reviewed record https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1080/00045608.2015.1072791].

The most reusable method is Sen et al.'s two-measure localness: about 5 million geolocated edits to geotagged articles in 39 editions, and 31.8 million cited URLs geolocated to publisher country with 91% accuracy. Editor localness ranged from 89.2% (Netherlands) to under 0.2% (Chad), source localness from 93.0% (Sweden) to under 0.2%; a country natively speaking the edition's language has 5.83× the odds of a local edit; the US supplied 61% of Arabic Wikipedia's cited sources while making under 1% of its edits [peer-reviewed, CHI 2015, https://brenthecht.com/publications/localnessgeography_CHI2015.pdf]. In purely rural US counties only 4% of article tokens come from locally focused editors and bot/batch content is 23.4% of tokens [peer-reviewed, CHI 2016, https://documentserver.uhasselt.be/bitstream/1942/20228/1/paper555.pdf]. A mean 23.2% of each of 40 editions is its own "cultural context content"; Georgian is not among the 40 [peer-reviewed, Frontiers in Physics 2018, https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fphy.2018.00054/full]. Georgia-specific localness values: **not found**.

The Sen et al. mechanism predicts high editor localness but possibly low source localness on kawiki (thin Georgian-language web publishing), and low editor localness with Russian or Western sources on ruwiki and enwiki Georgia articles, exactly the outsider-written condition at issue. Wikimedia's `geoeditors_monthly` dataset (editors by wiki × country × activity bucket) has a public bucketed release, but the endpoints were unreachable [practitioner, Wikitech, https://wikitech.wikimedia.org/wiki/Data_Platform/Data_Lake/Edits/Geoeditors]. Content Translation volumes into Georgian: **not found**; the counting API is documented [practitioner, mediawiki.org, https://www.mediawiki.org/wiki/Content_translation/Published_translations].

### Translation and language of origin

"Written in Georgian" cannot be equated with "written by a Georgian". Thompson et al. built a 6.4 billion-sentence multi-way parallel set from Common Crawl bitext in 90 languages: 57.1% of sentences with a translation sit in 3+-way parallel sets and 11.7% in 8+-way sets; the ten lowest-resource languages average 8.6-way parallelism against 4.0 for the top ten; 8+-way content scores 6.2 CometQE points lower, the signature of mass machine translation into low-resource languages. Georgian is among the 90 languages but no Georgian-specific number is printed [peer-reviewed, Findings of ACL 2024, https://aclanthology.org/2024.findings-acl.103.pdf]. HPLT v2 removes machine translation only by HTML-tag heuristics and measured 3.3% of English documents as MT with a register classifier [preprint, arXiv Mar 2025, https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.10267]; FineWeb-2 does not detect it at all.

Kreutzer et al.'s manual audit found Georgian once: WikiMatrix en-ka (41,638 pairs) had only 11.88% usable sentences among 100 audited, with 73.27% misaligned or mistranslated [peer-reviewed, TACL 2022, https://arxiv.org/pdf/2103.12028]. Mined Georgian bitext is therefore very noisy, and any insider/outsider classifier trained on it would inherit that noise. A Georgian translationese classifier or a Georgian MT-share figure: **not found**.

### Author-location proxies

Dodge et al. geolocated the hosting IPs of 175,000 C4 URLs: 51.3% US, 3.4% India, with CDNs and data centres as known distortions [peer-reviewed, EMNLP 2021, https://arxiv.org/pdf/2104.08758]. An LLM-extraction study found 18.7% ± 0.5% of Common Crawl documents contain a street address or coordinate pair [preprint, arXiv 2024, https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.04952]. GDELT's DOC 2.0 API exposes `sourcecountry` (outlet location) across 65 machine-translated languages [vendor claim, GDELT blog, https://blog.gdeltproject.org/gdelt-doc-2-0-api-debuts/]. At the macro level, the Data Provenance Initiative finds creator organisations in North America and Europe account for 85.5% of text datasets and 93.8% of text tokens, with Africa and South America under 0.2% of tokens and no meaningful improvement since 2013 [preprint, arXiv Dec 2024, https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.17847]. The curators, not just the writers, are outsiders. No source gives `.ge` sites by language or Georgian pages by hosting country: **not found**.

### A measurement pipeline

No published study measures the outsider-authorship share of content about Georgia or about any single small country. Combining the precedents above gives a four-stage pipeline:

1. **Define the "about Georgia" set.** Build an entity list from Wikidata (country = Q230, country of origin = Q230, citizenship = Q230, with ka/ru/en labels and aliases). Retrieve from FineWeb-2 rus_Cyrl, FineWeb English, FineWeb-2 kat_Geor and HPLT 3.0 every document passing an entity-density threshold (for example ≥2 distinct Georgia entities per 500 words or one in the title). Disambiguate against the US state and the language with a small classifier. Report counts per language; this denominator does not exist in any opened source.
2. **Score author origin per document** with independent proxies: `.ge` ccTLD; hosting country from the IP recorded in the Common Crawl index; RDAP registrant country; outlet-country lists (GDELT, a hand-built list of Georgian outlets including Russian- and English-language ones such as Georgia Today, Civil.ge, Interpressnews, Sova.news); in-text Georgian addresses or coordinates; by-line nationality where available. Classify as insider (Georgia-based), near-outsider (Russian/post-Soviet), far-outsider (Western/other) or unknown, and report inter-proxy agreement rather than a single label.
3. **Detect translated origin** following Thompson et al.: embed Georgian documents with LASER or SONAR, search for near-translations in the English and Russian sets, flag multi-way parallel clusters; conversely flag Russian or English Georgia documents that translate Georgian originals (insider content in an outsider language). Train a Georgian translationese classifier on Content Translation dumps (which carry source language) versus native Georgian news.
4. **Wikipedia layer.** For the 12,498 WikiProject Georgia pages, their ruwiki counterparts and the kawiki articles, pull revision histories, geolocate anonymous editors, attribute registered editors by declared location, geolocate cited URLs to publisher country, and compute editor and source localness exactly as Sen et al. did. Use the public Geoeditors files for ka and ru Wikipedia.

Report a 3×3 matrix (language of text × author-origin class) with document and token counts per corpus plus the translated-origin share; the outsider share of content about Georgia is then (near-outsider + far-outsider) ÷ classified, with the unknown share stated. An estimate, not a measurement: Russian-language "about Georgia" content in FineWeb-2 is on the order of hundreds of thousands to a few million documents (0.1–0.5% of 699 M Russian documents), comparable to or larger than the entire 3.7 M-document Georgian subset, most of which is not about Georgia-the-country at all.

## 2. Correction methods

This section gives the evidence for each method of bringing insider-written material into a model, with its costs and its Georgian coverage.

### Retrieval from insider-written sources

Retrieval is the most direct way to bring Georgian-authored text into answers without retraining, but it inherits language preference. Faux Polyglot found generators drew on native-language documents 42% of the time versus 29.58% for foreign-language ones, falling back in resource order (English 49.28% first, Arabic 39.77% last) when no query-language documents exist [peer-reviewed, NAACL 2025, https://aclanthology.org/2025.naacl-long.411/]. If a retrieved pool holds Russian and English passages about Georgia and few Georgian ones, the generator will lean on the Russian and English ones.

Two further cautions. In-language retrieval helped Russian QA (62.3 vs 53.9 with English retrieval) but hurt an unsupported language (Thai in Command-R: 10.1 vs 23.7), while prompting in the user language fixed answer language (Russian 41.7% → 96.5%) [peer-reviewed workshop, KnowLLM@ACL Aug 2024, https://aclanthology.org/2024.knowllm-1.15/]. Grounding improves facts, not cultural fluency: search grounding lifted Gemini's Ethiopia knowledge MCQ from 60.3% to 74.2% but significantly degraded stereotype avoidance, and human raters saw no effect on story fluency (p = .827) [preprint, arXiv Jul 2025, https://arxiv.org/html/2502.13497v4]. MIRACL has no Georgian [preprint, arXiv 2022, https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.09984v1]; the cost of building an insider-source corpus: **not found**.

### Reweighting training data

Sampling and mixture methods (XLM-R's α = 0.3 [peer-reviewed, ACL 2020, https://aclanthology.org/2020.acl-main.747.pdf]; UniMax [preprint, arXiv Apr 2023, https://arxiv.org/pdf/2304.09151]; DoReMi [preprint, arXiv Nov 2023, https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.10429]; Data Mixing Laws [preprint, arXiv Mar 2025, https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.16952]) give small, consistent gains and reduce tail-language overfitting, but every result concerns language identity or domain, never viewpoint. Under temperature 3.33 the lowest-resource languages are "repeated over 100 times" with visible overfitting; upweighting Georgian's 469 M CC-100 tokens beyond a few epochs hits that regime, and the Georgian text that exists is still what it is. Aya 23 chose depth over breadth and dropped Georgian [vendor/preprint, arXiv May 2024, https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.15032]. Reweighting by perspective or cultural source: **not found**.

### Continued pre-training on native corpora

Continued pre-training reliably raises target-language fluency at 5–50 B tokens and roughly 1k–16k GPU-hours, and every study that measured it found English regression: Llammas −3.1 MMLU [preprint, arXiv Apr 2024, https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/2404.04042]; EstLLM MMLU-Redux 69.6 → 63.9 and TruthfulQA 52.4 → 36.6 without recovery, on a roughly 100,000 GPU-hour budget [preprint, arXiv 2026, https://arxiv.org/html/2603.02041]; English perplexity 14.7 → 198.8 without replay [preprint, arXiv 2024, https://arxiv.org/html/2401.01055]; catastrophic forgetting on English XNLI at all BLOOM sizes [peer-reviewed, ACL 2023, https://aclanthology.org/2023.acl-long.653.pdf]. Replay of 1–5% for weak shifts and about 25% for language shifts is the main mitigation (−4.75 English points without replay vs −1.5 with 25%) [preprint, arXiv 2024, https://arxiv.org/html/2403.08763]. On a tight budget (8 B German tokens, about 226–230 H100-hours) adaptation made Mistral-7B worse in German and cost 15–18 English points [peer-reviewed workshop, WANT@ICML 2024, https://arxiv.org/pdf/2408.15793v1].

The regional analogues carry two warnings. The Armenian arm-gemma ablation found news-only continued pre-training "improves fluency but erodes knowledge" [preprint, arXiv 3 Sep 2026, https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.03350; abstract only]. AceGPT's Arabic adaptation raised Egypt-survey alignment (hard 30.60 vs 23.34) but lowered US alignment (soft 54.55 vs 63.90): the only indirect evidence that native-corpus training shifts perspective, and it arrived as forgetting [peer-reviewed, ACL 2024, https://arxiv.org/html/2402.13231]. Azerbaijani [vendor claim, AWS blog 28 May 2026, https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/machine-learning/training-azerbaijani-language-models-on-amazon-sagemaker-ai/] and Kazakh [vendor claim, ISSAI Dec 2024, https://issai.nu.edu.kz/kazllm/] efforts published no English or numeric evaluations.

The one Georgian-first LLM is Tbilisi AI Lab's Kona2: Mistral-Nemo continued on "about 30 billion tokens" of Georgian/English (split not given) with about 20K added Georgian tokens, then SFT and DPO [vendor claim, HF cards 2025, https://huggingface.co/tbilisi-ai-lab/kona2-12B-Base/blob/main/README.md; https://huggingface.co/tbilisi-ai-lab/kona2-12B]. No benchmark results, Georgian or English: **not found**. Frontier models score 64–83% on Georgian logical reasoning [peer-reviewed workshop, LowResNLP 2025, https://aclanthology.org/2025.lowresnlp-1.13.pdf]. Models of US, Chinese and European origin gave "remarkably similar patterns" of values, so a model's country of origin cannot be assumed to carry a national frame [preprint, arXiv Nov 2025, https://arxiv.org/html/2511.03980v1]. No continued-pre-training paper measured a perspective outcome; CPT on Georgian web text will not install a Georgian historical frame unless the corpus is curated for that content.

### Fine-tuning with culturally grounded data

Culture-specific fine-tunes are cheap and their general-ability cost is disputed. CultureLLM fine-tuned GPT-3.5 for about $6 per culture on roughly 500 WVS-derived samples and gained +8.1%, with over-fitting beyond 500 samples and no clear gain for Korean [peer-reviewed, NeurIPS 2024, https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10946]; CulturePark [peer-reviewed, NeurIPS 2024, https://arxiv.org/html/2405.15145], CultureBank [peer-reviewed, Findings of EMNLP 2024, https://arxiv.org/html/2404.15238] and CultureSPA [peer-reviewed, NAACL 2025, https://arxiv.org/html/2410.12971v1] report near-zero forgetting in their own checks. An independent replication found WVS-only adapters homogenised cultures (C-DIST 0.76–0.80) and cut MMLU from 45.38 to 42.78, and a narrative-only setting collapsed MMLU to 19.63 [preprint, arXiv 2025, https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.16408]. Stereotype amplification is unmeasured in all of them.

Georgia is absent from every culturally grounded dataset found, including the human-written Aya Dataset (Georgian appears only in the templated or translated Aya Collection, about 4.12 M rows) [preprint, arXiv Feb 2024, https://arxiv.org/html/2402.06619; vendor cards https://huggingface.co/datasets/CohereLabs/aya_dataset; https://huggingface.co/datasets/CohereLabs/aya_collection_language_split]. A "Georgia-CultureLLM" is feasible on cost, but survey-item tuning homogenises and Georgia is not in WVS Wave 7; it would need narrative and historical material.

### Preference tuning with native raters

Preference data is demographic- and country-dependent. PRISM (1,500 participants born in 75 countries, paid £9/hour for about 70 minutes) found model rankings shift by region and the top model preferred by only 45% [peer-reviewed, NeurIPS 2024 D&B best paper, https://arxiv.org/html/2404.16019]. Alignment *increased* Jensen–Shannon distance to national opinion distributions (Gemma-7B Japan 0.33 → 0.51), and RLHF "treats human variation as noise instead of signal" [peer-reviewed, ICML 2024, https://arxiv.org/html/2402.05070]. Durmus et al. attribute US-centric defaults partly to RLHF raters being "primarily North American" [preprint, arXiv 2024, https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.16388]. Multilingual DPO works on its own terms (54.4% win rate vs Aya-23-8B across 23 languages, no Georgian) [vendor/preprint, arXiv Jul 2024, https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.02552], with reward hacking after three iterative rounds [vendor claim, arXiv Dec 2024, https://arxiv.org/html/2412.04261]. Modular Pluralism's "community LMs" cut GlobalOpinionQA distance by 14.9% on average and a regional LM alone by 5.2–6.7%, while hurting US-centric OpinionQA [peer-reviewed, EMNLP 2024, https://arxiv.org/html/2406.15951].

The claim that Western raters pull outputs toward Western views is supported; the converse (Georgian raters pull outputs toward Georgian views) has no direct test: **not found**. No study used raters from Georgia or any Caucasus or Central Asian country.

### Prompting with perspective or cultural instructions

Cultural prompting is the cheapest lever and the most double-edged. Tao et al.'s "born in X and living in X" prompt improved value alignment for 71–81% of 107 countries but worsened it for 19–29%, over-correcting countries already near the default (Finland 0.20 → 2.43); Georgia's value sits in an unopened supplement [peer-reviewed, PNAS Nexus 17 Sep 2024, https://academic.oup.com/pnasnexus/article/3/9/pgae346/7756548]. Prompted as Russian, Durmus et al.'s model called sex between unmarried adults morally unacceptable 73.9% of the time against about 31% of actual Russians [preprint, arXiv 2024, https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.16388].

Asking in the target language does little by itself: correlations with national values were about 0.10 for local-language prompts alone, 0.28 with cultural framing added, and 0.53 for English plus cultural framing across 10 models and 11 countries (no Georgia), with Llama opening Russian-persona replies with "Comrade" [preprint, arXiv 6 Nov 2025, https://arxiv.org/html/2511.03980v1]. AlKhamissi et al. found a modest positive language effect for GPT-3.5 on Egypt [peer-reviewed, ACL 2024, https://arxiv.org/html/2402.13231]. Georgian-language queries will therefore not automatically elicit a Georgian frame.

### Knowledge editing

Parameter editing works in the edit language and fails across languages: ROME edited in English reached reliability 98.98 but generality fell to 26.65 in Chinese, and editing in Chinese cut reliability to 36.63 [peer-reviewed, ACL 2024, https://aclanthology.org/2024.acl-long.627.pdf]; MEMIT went from 60.73% in English to 7.18% in Chinese, with transfer better "within the same language family" [preprint, arXiv 2024, https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.04990; abstract only]. Retrieval-based editing transfers best but with portability under 10% and a 743-fact store [peer-reviewed, ACL 2024, https://aclanthology.org/2024.acl-long.21.pdf]; WISE claims an "impossible triangle" in lifelong editing [preprint, arXiv 2024, https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.14768]. Georgian is far from every tested language family, so editing is the least promising route. Editing on Georgian or on opinion rather than factual triples: **not found**.

### Where the methods conflict

In-language retrieval for an unsupported language was worse than English retrieval. Culture fine-tuning authors report about zero general-ability loss while the independent replication reports −2.6 MMLU and homogenisation. Vocabulary extension is described as harmful at a scale of tens of billions of tokens (the report gives no source for this), yet Kona2, Jais, SambaLingo and the Azerbaijani adaptation use it; the trade-off for Georgian script is untested. Prompt language has no effect on stance in Durmus and in Bulté and Rigouts Terryn, and a modest positive effect in AlKhamissi. Persona prompting improves value alignment for most countries (Tao) and degrades reasoning [Gupta et al., peer-reviewed, ICLR 2024, https://arxiv.org/html/2311.04892]. Georgian appears in the correction literature only as 469 M CC-100 tokens, a templated Aya Collection split, Aya-101 language support, a 100-question logic benchmark and the unevaluated Kona2.

## 3. Georgian sources

The table near the end of this brief lists each source with its size, licence, holder and URL. This section describes the overall picture and where the figures disagree.

### Volume sits outside Georgia; curation sits inside

The largest open, bulk-downloadable Georgian text is web-crawl derived and held outside Georgia: HPLT 3.0 (about 7.55 B tokens), CulturaX (about 2.6 B tokens), mC4 (about 2.5 B tokens), FineWeb-2 (about 1.44 B words) and HPLT 2.0 (about 1.24 B words), all under CC0 or ODC-By packaging licences that do not clear the copyright of the crawled pages [vendor/dataset cards, Hugging Face, checked 17–18 Oct 2026, https://huggingface.co/datasets/HPLT/HPLT3.0; https://huggingface.co/datasets/uonlp/CulturaX; https://huggingface.co/datasets/allenai/c4; https://huggingface.co/datasets/HuggingFaceFW/fineweb-2; https://huggingface.co/datasets/HPLT/HPLT2.0_cleaned]. These corpora overlap heavily, so their counts should not be summed. The commercial kaTenTen24 (869,908,570 words) is query-only [vendor claim, Sketch Engine checked 18 Oct 2026, https://www.sketchengine.eu/corpora-and-languages/georgian-text-corpora/].

Georgian-held, insider-curated collections are search-interface-only with unstated licences, or all-rights-reserved. The Georgian National Corpus holds "more than 202 million tokens" per a 2024 paper [peer-reviewed, Caucasus Journal of Social Sciences 2024, https://cjss.ug.edu.ge/index.php/cjss/article/download/292/278/1109] or "~217M words" per a community list [practitioner, awesome-geo-nlp checked 18 Oct 2026, https://github.com/alexamirejibi/awesome-geo-nlp]; its own page gives no total and names no licence, and the CLARINO record says "unspecified" [vendor, http://gnc.gov.ge/gnc/page; vendor, https://www.nb.no/sprakbanken/wp-json/wp/v2/language-resource/31561]. The 202 M vs 217 M gap most likely reflects continuous extension plus words-versus-tokens and could not be adjudicated because the primary statistics page was unreachable.

The GNC was built 2012–2019 by Goethe University Frankfurt (Jost Gippert) with the University of Bergen's CLARINO centre (Paul Meurer) and Georgian partners, funded by the Volkswagen Foundation: insider-authored text, technically outsider-held, with no bulk download or API stated. Ilia State's Georgian Language Corpus ("more than 100 000 000 word-forms", called "freely-available" in 2016, no licence on the site) [vendor, https://research.iliauni.edu.ge/en/project/33-georgian-language-corpus; peer-reviewed, EURALEX 2016, https://euralex.org/wp-content/themes/euralex/proceedings/Euralex%202016/euralex_2016_034_p328.pdf] and GTU's corpus.ge (270,886,730 word tokens as of 9 Sept 2017, no terms, funded at 200,000 GEL) [funder page, Rustaveli Foundation 17 Apr 2018, https://rustaveli.org.ge/eng/tsarmatebuli-proeqebi-da-mecnierebi/kidev-erti-nabiji-mosaubre-kartuli-tvitganvitarebadi-intelektualuri-korpusisaken] follow the same pattern.

### What this means for lawful use

For a retrieval system wanting Georgian insider perspective, the lawful-use set is: Wikipedia and Wikisource (CC BY-SA); OC Media (non-commercial); Geostat text; Tbilisi AI Lab datasets (mostly translated or synthetic); UD treebanks and AbNC; and crawl corpora under CC0/ODC-By packaging where the operator still bears copyright risk on source pages. News sites are generally silent or all-rights-reserved, and RFE/RL Georgian bans AI training. Insider-authored Georgian text that is both large and openly licensed barely exists; the gap between what Georgians have written and what can lawfully be retrieved or trained on is the central supply problem.

## 4. Whose perspective counts

### How NLP defines positionality

NLP has a well-cited body of work on whose view a dataset or model encodes, but its working definition of "insider" is implicit and demographic: an annotator represents a group if they self-report membership. NLPositionality re-annotated 600 instances with 16,299 labels from 1,096 annotators in 87 countries and found datasets and models align most with Western, White, college-educated and younger populations, with non-native English speakers among the least aligned [peer-reviewed, ACL 2023 Outstanding Paper, https://aclanthology.org/2023.acl-long.505/]. Santurkar et al. found misalignment with US groups "on par with the Democrat-Republican divide on climate change" that persists after steering [peer-reviewed, ICML 2023, https://proceedings.mlr.press/v202/santurkar23a.html]. GlobalOpinionQA's country labels mean the survey's national sample, qualified as "(Current national sample)" or "(Non-national sample)", not any self-identification [vendor claim, HF dataset card, https://huggingface.co/datasets/Anthropic/llm_global_opinions].

Annotator identity changes labels: more conservative annotators and those scoring high on a racist-beliefs scale were less likely to rate anti-Black language as toxic and more likely to rate African American English as toxic [peer-reviewed, NAACL 2022, https://aclanthology.org/2022.naacl-main.431/]; adding annotator demographics improves disagreement prediction [peer-reviewed, AAAI 2023, https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/26698]. The methodological response is perspectivism: keep conflicting labels in the gold standard, release non-aggregated data, and treat variation as signal [practitioner, Perspectivist Data Manifesto, https://pdai.info/]; multi-annotator models match or beat aggregated labels across seven tasks [peer-reviewed, TACL 2022, https://aclanthology.org/2022.tacl-1.6/]. Cambo and Gergle's "model positionality" work found that perspective clusters of annotators (silhouette 0.37) captured structure that demographic groupings (under 0.02) miss [preprint, arXiv 2022, https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.07031; venue secondary]. Documentation norms ask for annotator demographics: Data Statements' "Annotator Demographic" field [peer-reviewed, TACL 2018, https://aclanthology.org/Q18-1041.pdf] and the ACL Responsible NLP checklist item D5 [practitioner, ARR Oct 2024, https://aclrollingreview.org/responsibleNLPresearch/]. A dedicated "positionality statements in NLP" paper: **not found**; the nearest is a CHI 2025 scoping review of 272 HCI papers [peer-reviewed, CHI 2025, https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10205865/1/chi25-197.pdf].

### How cultural benchmarks recruit "insiders"

Across the opened benchmarks, insider means a country (sometimes a sub-national region) plus a self-reported filter. BLEnD requires native speaker, originally from the target region, and lived there "over half of their lifetimes"; three of its 16 units are sub-national (West Java, Assam, Northern Nigeria); where crowdsourcing was infeasible (Algeria, Korea, North Korea, Azerbaijan, West Java, Assam, Nigeria, Ethiopia) the authors directly recruited five annotators per region; average max-vote agreement was 3.16 of 5, and the authors "do not claim that our data fully represents all the speakers of any language/region" [preprint, arXiv Jan 2025, https://arxiv.org/html/2406.09948v2; venue secondary]. CulturalBench uses Prolific nationality plus primary residence before age 18, keeps a region only if at least 10 active Prolific annotators exist, and routes US/UK sub-groups by ethnicity or place (African American, Wales), the only sub-national crowd precedent found [preprint, arXiv Jun 2025, https://arxiv.org/html/2410.02677]. Aya's insider is only self-declared language proficiency and country of residence, with no native-speaker or origin requirement, unpaid volunteers, 68.1% male, and for 12 languages the five most active contributors wrote everything [preprint, arXiv Feb 2024, https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.06619]. PRISM records birth country (75) and residence country (38) separately, the only benchmark opened to do so [preprint, arXiv Dec 2024, https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.16019]. NormAd and GlobalOpinionQA have no insider annotators at all; NormAd's norms come from the Cultural Atlas, which the authors say "only captures the dominant cultural narrative present in each country" [peer-reviewed, NAACL 2025, https://arxiv.org/html/2404.12464].

Critiques are converging on the nation-as-culture proxy. A survey of more than 90 papers found none explicitly defines culture [preprint, arXiv Sep 2024, https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.15412]; "Hire Your Anthropologist!" reviews 20 benchmarks and recommends "involving cultural communities in design and validation" [preprint, arXiv Oct 2025, https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.05931]; an Indian regional study found only 39.4% of 515 questions agreed across five regions and models over-selected Central/North India by 30–40% [preprint, arXiv Apr 2026, https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.15550]; Hershcovich et al. state "Culture varies greatly within languages" and call for participatory involvement, without discussing diaspora [peer-reviewed, ACL 2022, https://aclanthology.org/2022.acl-long.482.pdf]. NLP work recruiting diaspora or heritage-speaker annotators to represent a homeland culture: **not found**.

### What data-governance work for low-resource communities advises

Community-issued guidance converges on decision rights, benefit flow, relationships before extraction, and the option to keep data closed. The CARE Principles (Collective Benefit, Authority to Control, Responsibility, Ethics, with twelve sub-principles) were drafted from an Indigenous-led workshop in Gaborone in November 2018 and published via GIDA in September 2019; they are designed to complement FAIR, and their authors frame the goal as being "FAIR and CARE" [peer-reviewed, Data Science Journal 4 Nov 2020, https://datascience.codata.org/articles/10.5334/dsj-2020-043; practitioner, GIDA one-pager Sep 2019, https://static1.squarespace.com/static/5d3799de845604000199cd24/t/5da9f4479ecab221ce848fb2/1571419335217/CARE+Principles_One+Pagers+FINAL_Oct_17_2019.pdf]. OCAP (Ownership, Control, Access, Possession) holds that First Nations alone control data collection in their communities, with possession as the practical mechanism for ownership [practitioner, FNIGC, https://fnigc.ca/ocap-training/]. Te Mana Raraunga's principles include Rangatiratanga (Māori control of their data), Manaakitanga (free, prior and informed consent) and Kaitiakitanga (Māori decide what stays restricted) [practitioner, TMR Oct 2018, https://static1.squarespace.com/static/58e9b10f9de4bb8d1fb5ebbc/t/5bda208b4ae237cd89ee16e9/1541021836126/TMR+Ma%CC%84ori+Data+Sovereignty+Principles+Oct+2018.pdf]. Te Hiku Media's Kaitiakitanga Licence requires permission before any access or use, explicit permission for commercial use, and warns that open sharing "could extend colonisation into the digital sphere" [practitioner, GitHub LICENSE.md, https://raw.githubusercontent.com/TeHikuMedia/Kaitiakitanga-License/master/LICENSE.md]. Local Contexts offers 20 TK Labels and 10 BC Labels that communities apply to state conditions for sharing [practitioner, https://localcontexts.org/labels/traditional-knowledge-labels/].

Masakhane's participatory framework (over 400 participants from at least 20 countries at the time of writing, 45 MT benchmarks, agendas voted democratically, contributors submitting via pull requests so they "experience ownership") [peer-reviewed, Findings of EMNLP 2020, https://aclanthology.org/2020.findings-emnlp.195.pdf] sits alongside a stated "data sovereignty" value: Africans should "decide what data represents our communities globally, retain ultimate ownership of that data, and know how it is used", together with openness and a rejection of "parachute research" [practitioner, masakhane.io, https://www.masakhane.io/]. In practice MasakhaNER data is CC BY-NC 4.0, a middle position [practitioner, GitHub, https://github.com/masakhane-io/masakhane-ner]. Steven Bird warns against treating "Indigenous knowledge as a commodity" and asks researchers to respect "the sovereignty of local people over their data" [peer-reviewed, COLING 2020, https://aclanthology.org/2020.coling-main.313/], and in 2024 recommends keeping a corpus closed and tied to its place while asking whether NLP can scale without open data [peer-reviewed, ACL 2024 Outstanding Paper, https://aclanthology.org/2024.acl-long.797/].

These positions conflict with open-by-default practice: Common Voice (CC0) [preprint, arXiv 2019, https://arxiv.org/pdf/1912.06670], ABNC (CC0), Aya (Apache 2.0) and ELRC-SHARE's recommendation of CC0/CC-BY/open-PSI licences [practitioner, ELRC, https://elrc-share.eu/info/]. UNESCO's 2021 Recommendation endorses both "a digital commons approach to data where appropriate" (para. 77) and Indigenous "self-governance of their data" (para. 47) [practitioner, UNESCO 23 Nov 2021, https://www.unesco.org/en/legal-affairs/recommendation-ethics-artificial-intelligence]. Mozilla's Data Collective now allows community-governed or compensated terms, a move away from CC0-only [vendor, https://mozilladatacollective.com/]. Consent signals on the open web are collapsing: within 2023–24, 5%+ of C4 tokens and 28%+ of the most actively maintained sources became fully robots.txt-restricted, and terms-of-service restrictions cover 45% of C4 [preprint, arXiv Jul 2024, https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.14933]; licence omission runs 70%+ and error rates 50%+ on hosting sites [preprint, arXiv Nov 2023, https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.16787]. The EU AI Act's Article 53 requires general-purpose model providers to publish training-content summaries and respect text-and-data-mining opt-outs from 2 August 2025 [practitioner, artificialintelligenceact.eu, https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/53/].

### Georgia's own legal and policy anchors

Georgia's Law on Personal Data Protection (No. 3144, adopted 14 June 2023) applies to controllers outside Georgia using technical means in Georgia, excludes processing for academic, artistic or literary purposes (Art. 2(2)(f)), permits processing of data the subject made public (Art. 5(1)(e)), and gives a right not to be subject to solely automated decisions (Art. 19); scraping is not addressed [practitioner (official text), matsne.gov.ge, https://matsne.gov.ge/en/document/view/5827307?publication=0]. Its main provisions took effect 1 March 2024, and the supervisory functions moved to the State Audit Office on 2 March 2026 [practitioner, DLA Piper tracker 13 Mar 2026, https://www.dlapiperdataprotection.com/index.html?t=law&c=GE]. The copyright law's limitation articles (21–28) could not be read and the Georgian text opened contained no text-and-data-mining terms; a TDM exception is **not verifiable** [practitioner, matsne.gov.ge, https://matsne.gov.ge/ka/document/view/16198]. No adopted national AI strategy or AI law was found as of 18 October 2026: **not found**; BTU published "Guiding principles" in October 2023 as an academic document [practitioner, BTU 26 Oct 2023, https://btu.edu.ge/wef-content/guiding-principles-of-the-national-strategy-of-georgia-on-artificial-intelligence/], the Economy Minister said a strategy is "in development" [practitioner, Imedi, https://imedi.ge/en/economy/10426/georgian-economy-minister-georgias-digital-transformation-aims-to-create-opportunities-ensure-every-citizen-benefits-from-digital-economy], and a Korea–Georgia digital-government cooperation centre (2026–2028) will advise on AI law [practitioner, fnnews 8 Sep 2026, https://en.fnnews.com/news/202609081728575834]. The academic-purpose exclusion and public-data permission create a wide lawful route for benchmark construction, but licensing of Georgian text should be checked source by source, and there is no Georgian AI strategy to cite as a governance anchor yet.
